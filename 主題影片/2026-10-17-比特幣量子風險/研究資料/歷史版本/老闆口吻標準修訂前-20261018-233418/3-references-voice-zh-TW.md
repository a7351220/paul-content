# Traditional Chinese Market House Voice

Profile ID: `market-house-voice-zh-tw-v1`

## Mode Changes Structure, Not Voice

Use this one voice for weekly reports and single-topic deep dives.

- Weekly mode covers more subjects, uses shorter setup, and needs clean topic breaks.
- Topic mode follows one question longer and may explain more prerequisites.
- Neither mode changes the speaker, beginner standard, sentence texture, source discipline, or banned patterns.
- Never turn weekly mode into a headline recital, news-anchor delivery, exaggerated host performance, or filler transitions.
- Never turn topic mode into a classroom lecture, report recital, or encyclopedia explanation.

For related weekly topics, bridge with the changed variable or market consequence. For unrelated topics, use a clean chapter break. Do not add `接下來我們來看`, `另一個值得關注的是`, or a fake question merely to simulate flow.

## Audience and Standard

Write for a beginner who understands basic investing but does not yet know the product map, industry shorthand, or sell-side workflow. Sound like someone who understands the market and is explaining it directly to a friend: straight, relaxed, patient, slightly masculine, and dense with useful information.

Do not sound like a research report, news anchor, classroom lecture, enthusiastic host, or someone deliberately acting familiar. `輕鬆` means the listener does not need to work hard to follow the logic. It does not mean adding banter, filler, slang, or empty questions.

Give each paragraph one clear job. Every judgment needs nearby evidence, but do not force every sentence to carry a number. Keep complete spoken paragraphs; do not chop sentences into fragments merely to sound forceful.

## Teaching Order

Organize beginner explanations around the investor question, not textbook completeness.

1. Show what the viewer is seeing now.
2. Name the tension or apparent contradiction.
3. State exactly what the viewer will learn to distinguish.
4. Introduce only the background and terms required for the next claim.
5. Move through evidence in causal or chronological order.
6. Return to the original investor question at the end.

Before drafting, identify the audience's likely unknowns and the order in which they must be introduced. Do not use a term merely because it appears in the research pack.

## Opening

Open a beginner teaching video with one visible market situation: results arrive, a price moves in both directions, two headlines disagree, or the same event produces different reactions.

The opening must establish the current situation, explain why it is hard to judge, and state the lesson. Keep the first two or three spoken sentences free of stacked earnings figures, product specifications, acronyms, and source qualifications. Use at most one exact number only when the scene cannot work without it. Put supporting figures on screen, in production notes, or in the next beat.

Keep the tone calm and warm. Do not manufacture interest with generic questions, exaggerated modifiers, promises, or fake familiarity.

## Definitions and First Use

Introduce every unfamiliar acronym, institution, product class, and price type before or at its first spoken use. Use the shortest phrase that removes the comprehension barrier.

- Pair a necessary acronym with a plain Chinese label once, then use the approved short form.
- Identify an institution only as far as its role in the claim requires, usually its country and role.
- Explain a product first by where it is used, who buys it, what demand or supply pressure matters, and why investors track it.
- Explain technical properties only when they change the investment conclusion.

Do not interrupt the story with an encyclopedia paragraph. If the listener can understand the next claim without the definition, omit it or move it to production notes.

## Data Pages

Make a number answer an investor question. A useful spoken data point makes clear:

- what product, company, or instrument it describes;
- whether it is actual, guidance, forecast, spot, contract, company average, or analyst assumption;
- the relevant period or comparison base;
- what changed;
- why that change matters to the page's conclusion.

Prefer `event or number -> cause or comparison -> supported judgment`. Do not restate a percentage in different words and call that an explanation.

Use before-and-after values, expectation gaps, or a defined market reaction when magnitude matters. A change inside one analyst model may explain how assumptions flow through a model; it does not by itself prove how the market behaved.

Keep secondary scope notes out of the spoken line when they do not change the conclusion. Preserve qualifications that prevent an overclaim.

## Titles

Use titles that remain understandable without the script body. Prefer a named subject plus its direction, change, conflict, or question. Do not use only abstract nouns, decorative modifiers, report jargon, or metaphors. Length is secondary to clarity. Repeated title structures are allowed when they help the viewer compare related products or cases.

## Source Names in Narration

Separate evidence provenance from spoken attribution.

- Put full titles, dates, links, page numbers, and source IDs in production notes.
- Name a source in narration when its identity is part of the claim: an analyst forecast, institutional framework, management statement, disputed estimate, or source conflict.
- Give the source the shortest useful identity on first mention.
- Do not repeat the source name before every supported sentence.
- Avoid report-language scaffolding when the fact can be stated directly.

Source density belongs in the evidence pack. Narration should carry the evidence, not recite the bibliography.

## Evidence Boundaries

Keep fact, forecast, interpretation, scenario, and method distinct.

Do not invent fixed observation windows, confirmation thresholds, multi-signal formulas, or conditions for calling a top, reversal, or buy signal. Any sentence shaped like `要連續觀察 N 期`, `出現 A 加 B 才算 C`, or `沒有 X 就尚未反轉` requires an explicit method source.

When no such source exists, describe only what the observed price or indicator represents, what direction it moved, and what remains unknown. Do not turn a reasonable personal heuristic into a market rule.

## Ending

End by answering the opening question in plain language. Then give the viewer one reusable way to avoid the specific mistake the video addressed.

Do not let the ending sound like an executive summary or compliance note. Do not introduce a new source, date, company, or number; repeat the same conclusion twice; hide the answer behind abstract language; or force a slogan, verdict, or call to action.

Prefer a concrete distinction, decision, or watch item over a general statement about the market.

## Global Editing Rules

When the user rejects a phrase, tone, definition pattern, or sentence structure, search the whole script for the same underlying habit. Do not repair only the quoted sentence.

Remove throat-clearing, fake conversational prompts, decorative modifiers, abstract conclusions without named evidence, repeated explanations, source attributions that belong in notes, technical detail that does not advance the investor question, and unsupported formulas presented as objective rules.

Preserve sourced numbers, dates, units, uncertainty labels, distinctions between price types and periods, natural Traditional Chinese investor language, user-approved wording, and qualifications needed to prevent a misleading conclusion.

A requested page conclusion should state the consequence of that page's new information. Preserve useful conclusions; remove repeated central claims. A later return to the same claim needs new evidence, a different everyday situation or a concrete choice, rather than another paraphrase. Expand with those additions while keeping the same one or two page-level points.

## Beginner Cold Read

Read the finished script as if none of the later slides exists. At each first use, verify that the listener can answer:

- What is this thing or institution?
- Where is the product used, or what role does the number play?
- Is this actual, forecast, spot, contract, company average, or analyst assumption?
- What changed, compared with what period or expectation?
- Why does it matter to revenue, profit, valuation, or price expectations?
- Which part is evidence, and which part is interpretation or uncertainty?

If the answer depends on information introduced later, reorder the explanation. If the definition adds no investment relevance, remove it. If a conclusion depends on a missing method or source, return a named evidence gap instead of smoothing it over.
